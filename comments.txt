This dissertation addresses fundamental theoretical limitations and trajectory generation challenges in autonomous system control, spanning network-level consensus dynamics to agent-level constrained path planning. First, we investigate velocity coupling bounds in double-integrator multi-agent systems over directed graphs, characterizing how the interplay of multiple directed loops induces consensus breakdown. To mitigate this instability, we propose a novel edge perturbation strategy tailored to loop-involved edges. This method guarantees consensus for predesigned coupling parameters through strictly localized network adjustments. Crucially, the formulation resolves the longstanding challenge where the root or leader node is embedded within a loop—a structural singularity that effectively converts all loop nodes into root nodes and renders standard hierarchical consensus algorithms obsolete.Expanding from topological stability to system performance, we conduct an $\mathcal{H}_2$ performance analysis for second-order agent networks on path graphs containing up to two loops. This analysis reveals that network robustness is governed not only by the relative placement of the loops to one another, but also by their spatial orientation relative to the root node, detailing key obstacles in extending performance bounds to general directed topologies.Finally, the research shifts to constrained motion control for individual agents requiring spatial and temporal precision. We first develop a Dubins-based path planning framework under lateral acceleration constraints, establishing simulation-backed design guarantees to satisfy exact terminal impact angle and impact time constraints simultaneously. To overcome geometric flexibility limitations inherent in fixed-radius primitives, we subsequently introduce an Archimedean spiral-based path synthesis strategy. By exploiting the additional degree of freedom offered by spiral parameterization, this approach expands the feasible trajectory space and delivers smooth curvature profiles under tight operational bounds. Together, these contributions advance the design of resilient, high-performance cooperative control and trajectory planning algorithms for complex autonomous systems.



This thesis is broadly structured into two complementary sections: \textbf{Section I: Analysis and Control}, which focuses on the structural vulnerabilities and stabilization of consensus networks subject to structural feedback loops, and \textbf{Section II: Design and Application}, which re-purposes looping kinematics for constrained spatio-temporal path planning. A fundamental theme of section I is the mathematical and dynamic distinction between the first-order (single-integrator) and second-order (double-integrator) multi-agent systems. This thesis can be broadly divided into two sections, namely, \textit{Section I: Analysis and Control}, which deals with particular emphasis of directed unintended loops in networked dynamical system of agents modeled as double integrator. It is interesting to note that while in networked systems of single integrators, the reverse edge leads to degradation of convergence rate, its effect in a network of double integrator may result in complete loss of consensus. The investigation of how to stabilize a system with such unintended loops preventing loss of consensus and moreover doing so by only utilizing local information will be the focus of this section. Analyzing effect of second reverse edge when one is already present in the system will be focus of the analysis part where a polynomial based approach is utilized to segregate Laplacian of the networked system in such a way that nodes and edges involved in cycle formation  are separated from nodes not involved in the loops in the system.

This thesis investigates consensus in networked dynamical systems with particular emphasis on the influence of directed cycles and their implications for stability and performance. For single-integrator agent dynamics, the introduction of reverse edges leading to directed cycle formation degrades the convergence rate to consensus, even when a directed spanning tree exists. In contrast, for double-integrator dynamics, the presence of directed cycles can lead to complete loss of consensus despite the existence of a directed spanning tree. This instability arises due to the requirement of a strictly positive lower bound on the velocity coupling gain, denoted by $\gamma$, introduced by reverse-edge feedback.

Existing polynomial-based Laplacian spectral analysis techniques enable estimation of admissible bounds on $\gamma$ without explicitly computing eigenvalues; however, these methods are limited to cases where the leader node is not part of the directed cycle. When the leader participates in the feedback loop, such approaches fail to provide reliable stability bounds, making systematic selection of a safe coupling gain challenging. This thesis develops several decentralized analytical tools to guarantee consensus for any pre-designed coupling gain in the presence of directed cycles. In addition to guaranteeing consensus when the leader is excluded from the cycle, a root-locus-based framework is proposed that ensures stability even when the leader is involved in the directed feedback structure.

The thesis further examines the structural role of cycles in undirected consensus networks of double-integrator agents from a performance perspective. While cycles are known to influence the Fiedler eigenvalue and, consequently, convergence rate, their effect on system performance metrics such as the $\mathcal{H}_2$ norm has not been comprehensively studied. It is first shown that $\mathcal{H}_2$ performance fundamentally depends on the underlying spanning tree structure. Using a path graph as the baseline spanning tree, the influence of cycle length and placement on performance is systematically analyzed. In particular, the interaction of two cycles is examined, demonstrating that longer cycles with partial overlap can yield improved $\mathcal{H}_2$ performance compared to non-overlapping configurations. The study also characterizes the extent of overlap that optimizes performance, thereby providing structural design guidelines for enhanced disturbance attenuation.

As an application of consensus principles, a trajectory planning framework is developed for impact-angle- and impact-time-constrained interception of a stationary target. The approach begins with a modification of the classical Dubins path, wherein the desired terminal constraints are satisfied by appropriately selecting the radius of a design circle. The admissible range of this radius is analytically characterized as a function of the initial engagement geometry. To overcome the rigidity inherent in fixed-radius Dubins trajectories, a spiral-based guidance strategy is introduced as a flexible extension. The spiral guidance law allows continuous adjustment of curvature, thereby enlarging the feasible solution space and improving robustness against geometric and acceleration constraints. The proposed strategy requires only piecewise-constant lateral acceleration over distinct engagement phases, making it practical for implementation. A closed-loop realizable form of the guidance law is also presented, and simulations demonstrate its superiority over conventional methods.

Finally, the trajectory planning framework is extended to multi-agent interception scenarios by integrating the spiral-based guidance strategy with consensus principles. This extension enables coordinated multi-agent engagement while maintaining prescribed impact-angle and timing constraints. The resulting unified framework bridges consensus theory and cooperative guidance design, providing both rigorous stability guarantees and practical trajectory generation strategies for coordinated autonomous systems.
